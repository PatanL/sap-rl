# First training-signal test

I would change the target at the last decision of a completed shop turn. Estimate **the outcome-averaged one-step match return**, including the value of the resulting shop. Keep one real fight for settlement and play the actual trajectory onward. This is one experimental change, with historical learning rates, head coefficients, lag and opponents held fixed. The plateau motivates the test; it does not identify combat randomness as its cause.

For the actual two post-END teams, estimate win/draw/loss probabilities with 16 outcomes, including the actual fight and 15 independently seeded repetitions. Maintain the engine’s actual paired world, shop RNG stream and battle seed. For each observed outcome class, apply the real settlement rule to a private clone with that outcome forced. Run next-turn triggers on that clone, preserving legal public information, and bootstrap each nonterminal seat with its own last valid GRU state. Then form the detached scalar

`Y = sum_o p[o] * (r[o] + discount[o] * V(public_next[o], h_end))`.

Here `r[o]` includes the exact 0.1 life-swing arithmetic, turn-two recovery and signed terminal bonus from that branch. Terminal branches have zero bootstrap. Wins, lives, the game's own future, the network observation and the hard factual battle label still follow the actual outcome. Common future-shop randomness across clones is useful when each branch has the correct marginal transition. Do not reuse a post-win shopping state to value a hypothetical loss. In the learner, the replacement can be implemented as a detached final-step correction `Y - (r_actual + discount_actual * V_actual)` in the target calculation for both the critic and policy advantage. Preserve V-trace importance ratios and keep factual reward and discount in storage for audit. A one-sample implementation must reproduce the current target and gradient.

### Resource budget: work estimates, not invented learner rates

Using the supplied 64,000 batched native fights/core-second, one extra fight per boundary costs 15.6 μs of one core; 15 extras cost **234 μs/boundary**. The earlier full collector proof observed 104.4 fights/s (0.040 fights per seat decision), giving 0.0245 dedicated CPU core at that *historical* boundary rate for 15 extras. At F current boundaries/s the corresponding budget is `15F/64000` cores. Dispatch inside Rust in batches; crossings and copying snapshots can dominate the fight arithmetic. These are ideal CPU service budgets, not wall-time or decisions/s predictions.

The M2/M16 immediate-reward-only and soft-WDL variants require **zero extra learner forwards**; E requests no experiments, so g’s throughput penalty is unmeasured. The expected full target needs **at most two extra value-only rows per eligible live seat-turn**, irrespective of 16 battle samples, reusing the factual bootstrap and skipping terminal or unsampled branches. The long run averaged 12.59 learned decisions per live turn, making the upper bound **0.159 additional forward-only rows per learned decision** on that distribution; there is no extra backward pass per counterfactual and no new optimizer step. That is not a 15.9% training slowdown. Packing, GPU contention, branch settlement and transport have to be timed. For scale, 8,192 decisions at 2,693/s take 3.04 s. Keeping at least 90% of that throughput permits only 0.34 s of additional uncovered wall time per update. At the two-extra-row upper bound (about 1,301 rows/update), fully serialized branch-value inference alone would have to exceed roughly 3,850 rows/s, leaving further margin for transport and cloning. This is a break-even requirement, not a measured native VF rate. Full counterfactual *match rollouts* would instead require fresh policy inference on future shops at every branch and are a much more expensive estimator.

### Fixed-budget decision

1. Before the GPU test, replay held development trajectories with M1: require identical public transitions, actual seed and outcome, reward, recurrent bootstrap and optimizer step. Check turn-two recovery, wins/lives, ties, turn cap and terminal ±2 on forced outcomes. Log variance of the *full boundary target* across sampled battle seeds separately from intermediate-reward variance.
2. Two matched training seeds, each with a historical M1 control and a target-only treatment, all from the identical 25,772 weights and Adam moments. Keep both policies’ frozen mixture, decision target 8,192 and max lag 1. Use the same candidate-selection-free update horizon across arms (e.g. 80 updates with a six-minute hard budget per arm). Compare the same complete update across a pair if a cap intervenes. Record accepted decisions/s, stale share, learner and branch-value time, target variance, scalar value error on disjoint self-play matches, and calibration. Predefine a 10% allowable throughput loss for this screen.
3. Allow up to 24 GPU minutes for the four arms, about 4 minutes for two direct treatment/control comparisons at batch **4,096**, 2,048 seat-swapped pairs per seed, and up to 26 minutes for four matched 1,000-game ranked-board recordings at their established **128** greedy batch (four times the supplied 380-second cost). Leave six minutes for loading and contingencies. No watcher promotion; record all four arms. Compare head-to-head by entire worlds and the external board delta by whole self-play matches, reporting the two training seeds separately. A replicated, positive direct result with no material external loss justifies longer testing; an index or strength gain inside its interval remains inconclusive. This is a screening experiment under an hour, not a powered claim about a three-point long-run gain.

### Separate decision: ranked boards as opponents

Only with user approval, partition fixtures by the original match-ID hash first. Build a turn-indexed pool **solely** from the training side (`sha256(match_id) % 5 >= 2`), log fixture IDs, and assert disjointness from the yardstick. Sample an opponent by turn and preregister the mixture alongside self-play. In a ghost match one live policy shops, then its true post-END board fights the sampled board, with lives/wins settled under the same rules. Return only opponent information visible after the fight; the current draw and future boards stay hidden during shopping. Learn only from the live seat’s rewards and log probabilities. A ranked ghost is a board-generating opponent, not an adaptive human playing a match. This replaces the other seat’s shop and policy inference with a board lookup and battle. Each ghost match supplies one learner seat. Keeping other game widths fixed can reduce eligible seats relative to a two-live mirror, while removing opponent inference can speed acting. The learner graph and cost per accepted row stay the same; the net learned decisions/s depends on the mix and needs measurement. Keep measurements on the untouched yardstick side and on fixed-batch self-play. There is no imitation loss, human action target, scripted purchase or manually preferred combo.
